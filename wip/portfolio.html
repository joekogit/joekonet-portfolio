Case Study 01Atlas Design System · Peacock / NBCU
2023 — 2025
NEW YORK, NY

Case study · Peacock / NBCUniversal · 2023—2025

Atlas, scaled from one brand to four.

A flagship streaming design system asked to become a multi-brand foundation. We rebuilt it as a tiered token architecture and shipped themed UI in days instead of weeks.

RoleSenior Design Engineer · Atlas DS
TeamMulti-disciplinary DS pod · 6+
ScopeWeb · client/TV · 4 brands · global
StakesNBCU's Global Streaming Platform

Context

Peacock launched on a mature design system tuned for one flagship streaming product. Then NBCU's Global Streaming Platform strategy turned Peacock's tech into the foundation for SkyShowtime, NOW, and Showmax — partner services in different markets, with different brand expressions, on the same plumbing.

Atlas wasn't a system any more. It was a platform dependency for an international product line. And the clock was running.

The brief, in plain English. Stop maintaining one design system. Start maintaining a foundation that can produce four design systems on demand — without the brands fragmenting, drifting, or stepping on each other.
The thinking →
RejectedA separate library per brand. Cheap to start, impossible to govern. Drift would have eaten the program inside two quarters.
RejectedA single component that knew about every brand. Conditional spaghetti that hides intent and slows engineers down.
Bet onA tiered token model: base → semantic → component. Components see only semantics. Brands swap base values. The system stays one thing.
AndNaming conventions that mapped to engineering output, so the token didn't change shape between Figma and the pipeline.
Margin note —
The hardest sell wasn't the architecture. It was getting four brand teams to agree which values were brand-owned and which belonged to the system. That's a politics problem dressed as a technical one.

Case Study 02Design QA Program · Peacock
2024
NEW YORK, NY

Case study · Peacock / NBCUniversal · 2024

Design QA, made boring on purpose.

UI quality issues kept landing late. I built a Design QA program that turned ad-hoc Slack feedback into a tracked, two-week, before-release ritual — and reduced late-cycle rework by 30–50%.

RoleProgram lead · Design Engineer
PartnersDesign · Eng · PM · Functional QA
ScopeWeb · native · multi-platform
StakesRelease predictability + design debt

Context

As feature velocity climbed, fidelity slid. Functional QA caught the does it work bugs. Nobody had time, structure, or authority for the does it look right, behave right, read right, work for everybody bugs.

Designers got 2–3 days at the end of a sprint to review builds. Most "approvals" were conditional. Decisions lived in Slack threads that disappeared. Figma drifted from production. Design debt compounded silently, every release.

The actual problem.Design QA wasn't being skipped because people didn't care. It was being skipped because it had no infrastructure — no checklist, no Jira status, no calendar window, no Definition of Done line item.
The thinking →
Rejected"Designers should just review more carefully." A request, not a process. Will not survive the next release.
RejectedA pixel-perfect mandate. Stops shipping. Burns engineering goodwill in a week.
Bet onDesign QA as ops infrastructure: a checklist, a Jira workflow, a defined two-week window, co-authored with engineering so they own it too.
AndPitching it as rework reduction, not perfection. The metric was "fewer late surprises," not "more bug tickets."
Margin note —
Co-authoring the checklist with engineers was the unlock. Once they'd written half of it, it stopped being a designer's wishlist and became a shared definition of done.

Issues triaged across visual, accessibility, copy, interaction. Each carries a fidelity definition co-owned by design and engineering.

Case Study 03Rapid Prototyping · Commerce
2024 — 2025
NEW YORK, NY

Case study · Peacock Commerce · 2024—2025

Prototypes that make a decision.

High-fidelity, state-aware, AI-accelerated. Modeled annual-vs-monthly, tier swaps, four-brand themes — so commerce decisions happened on the artifact, one to two sprints earlier than they used to.

RoleDesign Engineer · prototypes
ToolsFigma Variables · React · Claude Code · Cursor · MCP
SurfacesWeb paywall · client/TV · billing · promos
StakesPre-engineering decisions on real flows

Context

Commerce flows have state. Annual vs monthly. Tier × package. Region. Brand theme. Promo overrides. Price-formatting rules. Static comps couldn't communicate any of it. Every decision meeting started with five minutes of "wait, but what about…" — about behavior nobody could see.

The prototype became the meeting agenda.

The frame I worked from.A prototype isn't a deliverable. It's a decision instrument. Its job is to make the room agree, in one session, on something that would otherwise take three.
The thinking →
RejectedClick-through Figma frames. Fine for happy path; useless for the conditional logic that actually contains the decisions.
Bet onVariables-driven Figma for fast iteration; code prototypes when interaction realism demanded it. Match the tool to the question.
Bet onAI tooling — Claude Code, Cursor, Figma MCP — as a force multiplier on scaffolding, not a replacement for design judgment.
GuardrailEvery AI-generated scaffold gets grounded in the design system. Prototypes had to behave like production constraints, not design ideal.
Margin note —
Early adoption, not because it was fashionable, but because Commerce had more state-permutations than I could prototype by hand inside a Shape Up cycle. It was a math problem.
Fig. 3.1Plan picker · four states.

One component, four real product configurations. Billing-cycle toggle, brand theme, locale + currency, and a regional promo override — each one a Variable swap, no fork. Tap a tab to switch state.

Case Study 04Particle UI · Amount
2020 — 2023
CHICAGO, IL

Case study · Amount · 2020—2023

Particle UI, treated like an internal SaaS.

Without a dedicated PM, I ran our component library and theming system as a real product — multi-cycle Shape Up roadmap, governance, accessibility productized — so partner banks could ship branded UI without reinventing primitives.

RoleManager, Design Systems Engineering
Team3 → 6 UI engineers
CustomersCapital One · Jenius/SMBC · Guaranteed Rate
StakesEnterprise-deal blocking infrastructure

Context

Amount spun out of Avant and pivoted from direct-to-consumer to B2B SaaS sold to banks. Particle UI — our component library — went from "the thing the lending product is built on" to "the thing every partner deal depends on." Same library, very different politics.

Three problems landed at once: scaling an engineering team, running the program with no PM, and making accessibility a sales-enablement capability rather than a checkbox. I owned all three.

The frame.Stop treating Particle UI like a support function. Treat it like an internal SaaS platform with consumers, a roadmap, a contract, and a sales pitch — because that's what it actually was.
The thinking →
Move 01Multi-cycle Shape Up pitches, not sprints. Made scope, success criteria, and sequencing legible to non-DS stakeholders for the first time.
Move 02Productized accessibility into a tiered offering. Audits, remediation, vendor coordination — turned a compliance tax into a revenue-generating capability that unblocked enterprise deals.
Move 03Hired and mentored. Scaled the team from 3 to 6, with leads-within-the-team so the program wasn't me-shaped.
Move 04Initiated DS governance: ownership, decision cadence, contribution pathways. Aligned what was in Figma with what shipped.
Margin note —
Productizing accessibility was the surprise unlock. Once procurement at Capital One saw the audit cadence as a contractual deliverable, the conversation shifted from "do you do a11y" to "how fast."
"A clearer, more predictable delivery model and a design system platform that progressed from internal support work into a productized capability."
— STAR · Amount · Product Owner

§04
How I workSix principles, in plain English
Process
Collaboration

Calm under pressure. Specific in feedback. Allergic to ceremony.

/01

I work at the bridge.

Designers know I read their files; engineers know I read their PRs. The fastest way to lose a fidelity argument is to be the loudest person in the room with the least context. I bring context.

/02

Decisions over deliverables.

The artifact's job is to make the decision. A prototype that doesn't change anyone's mind was a misallocation of your week. I default to "what's the smallest prototype that decides this?"

/03

Operationally serious.

I run programs the way a PM runs programs. Roadmap, definition of done, durable doc, calendar slot. Ad-hoc is a great way to look busy and ship nothing.

/04

AI is a tool, not a worldview.

Early adopter — Claude Code, Figma MCP, Cursor — because Commerce had more state-permutations than I could prototype by hand. Outputs are always grounded in system standards, never released raw.

/05

Mentor by default.

Scaled the Amount team 3 → 6 by promoting leads-within-the-team. The point of a senior person isn't to be the bottleneck. It's to remove the need for one.

/06

Outside-my-lane willing.

Wrote the Shape Up pitches. Ran the accessibility vendor relationship. Did sales-enablement collateral when procurement asked. Senior is a stance, not a title.
